# V7 world political sequences and source review

Research date: 2026-10-23. Added **37 bilingual political overviews** in `dist/data/world-depth-v7.json`, including seven modern continuity/constitutional rows. Each entry has two narrative paragraphs per language, two key facts per language, qualified dates, a representative location, specific source/evidence notes, related reading and explicit major-timeline metadata.

This is a bounded expansion of political sequences, not parity with the 975 imported Chinese events. No new historical boundary geometry is inferred from these records. The new timeline can use political intervals without putting site histories, biographies and every single event on the same layer.

## Editorial choices and evidence limits

- Dynastic, constitutional and site periods are distinct. The Hoysala, Kamakura and Muromachi notes explain why academic chronologies use different start dates. The Satavahana record is explicitly the later c. 50–230 CE sequence used by Harvard, without guessing an early founding date.
- Several entries are plural lineages, not unitary empires: Mixtec kingdoms, Champa and branched Qarakhanids. Toltec Tula uses the documented urban/political center phase and does not pretend that shared imagery establishes imperial control.
- Medieval endpoints are qualified. Makuria uses a rounded archaeological-period convention. Pala uses the McMullen collection period while noting disagreement about the last local rulers. Qajar uses Amanat’s 1786–1925 convention and discloses the Met’s different art-period dates.
- Modern rows show state continuity or an explicitly named constitutional phase; they do not imply fixed borders, unchanged rights or one uninterrupted regime. The USSR ends in 1991. Japan’s 1947 row is the postwar constitutional order, not the start of Japan as a country.
- The main sources are museum research, university scholarship, archaeological institutions, archival documents and official institutional histories. Government accounts are used for dated documents and institutional transitions; their framing is not treated as neutral proof of all historical interpretations.
- Full-page retrieval was blocked for the British Museum Myanmar article, the University of Warsaw project page, the Library of Congress teaching collection, and the French defense archive page. Indexed institutional text and accessible companion scholarly sources were used; the source notes identify these limits. The Pala/Sena PDF was retrieved but its text extractor returned no text.
- Chinese narratives are original companion translations of the source-grounded synthesis. No copied source passages, new historical images or boundary polygons were imported.

## Record and source register

### Later Satavahanas: regional kingship in the Deccan

ID: `satavahana-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 50–230. Region: south-asia.

c. 50–230 CE follows Harvard’s later Satavahana ruler sequence. This is an explicit later-dynasty political phase; earlier Satavahana origins are disputed and are not assigned an invented founding date here. The pin is a Deccan regional reference, not a permanent capital.

- [South Asia, 1000 B.C.–1 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/04/ssa.html) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Describes emergence of the Satavahanas after Mauryan disintegration and their Deccan setting.
- [Satavahana-Saka-Kushan Age](https://timelinesouthasianhistory.hsites.harvard.edu/south-asia-satavahana-saka-kushan-age-c-1-%E2%80%93-300-ce) — Harvard University — A Timeline of South Asian History. Separates concurrent regional dynasties and dates later Satavahana rulers c. 50–230 CE.

### Pala: Buddhist courts in Bengal and Bihar

ID: `pala-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 750–1174. Region: south-asia.

Approximate Pala-period convention c. 750–1174 follows the McMullen Museum. Surveys differ over early consolidation and whether late local rulers belong to the imperial line; the Met uses a broader c. 700–1200 grouping. This is qualified political chronology, not Nalanda’s lifespan.

- [South Asia: North, 500–1000 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/06/ssn) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Describes Pala patronage of Nalanda and Vikramashila, with a broad dynastic period.
- [Bengal under the Palas and Senas (750–1204)](https://pjhc.nihcr.edu.pk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/2-Bengal-under-the-Palas-and-Senas750-1204Syed-Umar-Hayat.pdf) — Pakistan Journal of History and Culture / NIHCR. Scholarly survey of Pala and subsequent Sena political history; PDF retrieved but text extraction unavailable.
- [Northeastern India, Pala period (750–1174)](https://mcmullenmuseum.bc.edu/ne-india/) — McMullen Museum of Art, Boston College. Institutional collection record uses the Pala period 750–1174; this is a qualified period convention, not a claim that all late local rulers belong to the imperial line.

### Rashtrakuta: a Deccan power among regional rivals

ID: `rashtrakuta-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 750–973. Region: south-asia.

Approximate dynasty c. 750–973 follows the Met. The representative point near Manyakheta does not imply a fixed territorial outline or that every Ellora monument belongs to this dynasty.

- [South Asia: North, 500–1000 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/06/ssn) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Dates Rashtrakuta rule c. 750–973 among concurrent regional dynasties.
- [Ellora Caves](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/243/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Describes the Buddhist, Hindu and Jain complex and its long building sequence.

### Hoysala: temples as a language of regional kingship

ID: `hoysala-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1026–1343. Region: south-asia.

Qualified dynastic convention 1026–1343; earlier chiefly origins and terminal successor politics yield other dates in surveys. The point marks Halebidu, not the kingdom’s entire extent. The cited UNESCO ensembles are principally twelfth–thirteenth century.

- [Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1670/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Assesses the three temple complexes and deliberate differentiation from neighboring kingdoms.
- [Corporeal Experience and the Material World of the Vijayanagara Sacred Landscape](https://www.academia.edu/64118995/Corporeal_Experience_and_the_Material_World_of_the_Vijayanagara_Sacred_Landscape_600_1325_CE) — Candis L. Haak / University of Toronto doctoral dissertation (2018). Author’s dissertation text and political chronology table use Hoysala kingdom 1026–1343 and identify Dvarasamudra; consulted via the author’s Academia-hosted full text.

### Kakatiya: reservoirs, temples and a Telugu-speaking court

ID: `kakatiya-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1123–1323. Region: south-asia.

1123–1323 follows UNESCO’s Kakatiya-period convention; some political histories include earlier subordinate rulers. Warangal is a representative center. The temple’s 1213 start is distinct from the dynasty’s start.

- [Kakatiya Rudreshwara (Ramappa) Temple](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1570/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Gives the period 1123–1323, 1213 temple foundation, reservoir setting, construction materials and dance imagery.

### Sikh kingdom: uniting power in the Punjab

ID: `sikh-kingdom-punjab`. Displayed interval: 1799–1849. Region: south-asia.

1799–1849 follows the British Museum and the Cambridge dynastic chapter. Ranjit Singh’s capture of Lahore anchors the start; his royal coronation was later. The point is Lahore, not the kingdom’s changing borders.

- [Sikh](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x103722) — British Museum. Identifies the independent Punjab kingdom 1799–1849 under Ranjit Singh.
- [The Sikh empire (1799–1849)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/sikhs-of-the-punjab/sikh-empire-17991849/F392B4CF5D691DA781A20C00CCF5DC2A) — J. S. Grewal / Cambridge University Press. Scholarly chapter defining the political interval.

### Samanids: government and Persian culture at Bukhara

ID: `samanid-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 819–999. Region: west-central-asia.

819–999 represents the dynastic governorships through the loss of Bukhara and partition. A claimant resisted until 1005, so 999 is not the disappearance of every Samanid claimant. Bukhara is a representative court point.

- [Central and North Asia, 500–1000 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/06/nc.html) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Describes Samanid autonomous control and Persian cultural patronage.
- [Khorasan in the Taherid and Samanid periods](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khorasan-vi-history-in-the-taherid-and-samanid-periods/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Examines government, irrigation offices, documents and the end of Samanid control.
- [Abd-al-Malek b. Nuh](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abd-al-malek-b-nuh-b-mansur-samanid-ruler-in-khorasan-and-transoxania-r-999/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Dates loss of Bukhara and division of the dominions to 999.

### Ghaznavids: a court between Iran and South Asia

ID: `ghaznavid-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 977–1186. Region: west-central-asia.

977–1186 follows Bosworth’s dynastic definition. Ghazni is the early court reference; the final rulers were in Lahore. The interval does not assert unchanged control across the whole region.

- [Ghaznavids](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ghaznavids/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Defines 977–1186 and explains the dynasty’s changing geography.
- [Khorasan from the Ghaznavids to the Mongol conquest](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khorasan-vii-history-from-the-ghaznavids-to-the-mongol-conquest/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Dates the loss of Khorasan and the final fall of Lahore.

### Ghurids: a mountain dynasty becomes an imperial power

ID: `ghurid-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1150–1215. Region: west-central-asia.

Approximate c. 1150–1215 represents the mid-twelfth-century major-power phase, not the beginning of the local family. Earlier chiefly origins are excluded. Jam is a regional reference; several courts and campaign centers were involved.

- [Ghurids](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ghurids/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Explains mountain origins, mid-twelfth-century expansion, Persian literature and demise by 1215.
- [Minaret and Archaeological Remains of Jam](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/211/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Identifies Jam’s Ghurid historical context and late-twelfth-century monument.

### Qarakhanids: Turkic Muslim courts across Central Asia

ID: `qarakhanid-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 950–1212. Region: west-central-asia.

Approximate c. 950–1212 uses the mid-tenth-century emergence of the Muslim dynasty through the western branch’s end. Earlier clan origins and the eastern branch have different dates. Samarkand is one representative center.

- [Qarakhanid](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/qarakhanid/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Defines the dynasty’s mid/late-tenth to early-thirteenth-century span and documents source limitations and branches.

### Golden Horde: a Mongol successor state across the steppe

ID: `golden-horde`. Displayed interval: 1227–1502. Region: west-central-asia.

1227–1502 follows the Met’s broad dynastic convention, ending with the Great Horde; the western conquests and fragmentation occurred within that span. The Sarai-area point is approximate and does not fix a single capital for all years.

- [The Legacy of Genghis Khan](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-legacy-of-genghis-khan) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Identifies the Mongol successor states and dates Golden Horde c. 1227–1502.
- [Golden Horde](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/golden-horde/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Scholarly history of the Jochid western realm, political geography and fragmentation.

### Qajar: royal revival and changing public images

ID: `qajar-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1786–1925. Region: west-central-asia.

1786–1925 follows Amanat’s Qajar-period convention; other art surveys use 1779–1924. The interval includes initial consolidation and later constitutional monarchy, not a single unchanged regime. Tehran is a representative capital.

- [Nineteenth-Century Iran: Continuity and Revivalism](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/nineteenth-century-iran-continuity-and-revivalism) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Examines old royal references and newer media; uses an explicitly different art-period convention.
- [Nineteenth-Century Iran: Art and the Advent of Modernity](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/nineteenth-century-iran-art-and-the-advent-of-modernity) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Discusses photography, naturalism and changing painting.
- [Historiography: Qajar period](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/historiography-viii/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Abbas Amanat explicitly uses 1786–1925 and examines changing court and constitutional historical writing.
- [Ahmad Shah Qajar](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ahmad-shah-qajar-1909-1925-the-seventh-and-last-ruler-of-the-qajar-dynasty/) — Encyclopaedia Iranica. Identifies the last ruler and deposition in 1925.

### Merovingians: Frankish kingdoms after Roman Gaul

ID: `merovingian-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 482–751. Region: europe.

482–751 follows the Met chronology, anchored to Clovis’s reign and the replacement of the last Merovingian king. Earlier family origins are excluded; the kingdoms were divided and reunited within this span.

- [France, 500–1000 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/06/euwf) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Dates Merovingian rule 482–751 and explains Roman continuities, conversion and monasteries.

### Carolingians: royal power, learning and partition

ID: `carolingian-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 751–987. Region: europe.

751–987 follows the western Frankish dynastic sequence, not a united empire lasting to 987. The imperial title began in 800 and partitions changed authority earlier. Aachen is one representative royal center.

- [Carolingian Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/carolingian-art) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Connects coronation, classical learning, court scholars and artistic production.
- [France, 500–1000 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/06/euwf) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Gives western dynastic dates 751–987.

### Ottonians: kings and an imperial revival

ID: `ottonian-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 919–1024. Region: europe.

919–1024 includes Henry I’s kingship before Otto I; the Met art chronology often starts at Otto’s 936 coronation. Imperial rule starts in 962. Magdeburg is a representative patronage center, not a constant capital.

- [Ottonian Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/ottonian-art) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Explains Otto I’s 936 and 962 coronations, church patronage and imperial references.
- [The dynastic changes of 751 and 919 in contemporary sources](https://utheses.univie.ac.at/detail/13983) — University of Vienna. Scholarly source study identifies the 919 dynastic transition.

### Capetians: assembling royal power in France

ID: `capetian-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 987–1328. Region: europe.

987–1328 represents the direct Capetian royal line; cadet branches ruled later. Paris is a representative center, not the extent of royal control in every year.

- [France, 1000–1400 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/07/euwf) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Provides direct dynastic dates, regional lords and growth of royal authority.

### Dutch Republic: provinces, commerce and empire

ID: `dutch-republic`. Displayed interval: 1581–1795. Region: europe.

1581–1795 uses abjuration through replacement by the Batavian Republic. Some surveys start the republic in 1588; international recognition followed in 1648. The Hague is a representative governmental point.

- [From Rebellion to Republic](https://academic.oup.com/british-academy-scholarship-online/book/51415/chapter/417810872) — Helmer Helmers / British Academy — Oxford University Press. Examines 1581 Act of Abjuration and the republic’s institutional emergence.
- [In Praise of Painting: exhibition guide](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/in-praise-of-painting-dutch-masterpieces/visiting-guide) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Connects Dutch painting to independence and the seventeenth-century republic.
- [Batavia’s Company capital](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/09/sse.html) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Regional chronology of Dutch colonial power in Southeast Asia.
- [Popular celebrations marking the Batavian Revolution, 1795](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/399142) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Institutional record of a drawing of the 1795 political transition.

### Nasrids: Granada between diplomacy and conquest

ID: `nasrid-granada`. Displayed interval: 1232–1492. Region: europe.

1232–1492 follows the Met’s dynastic convention. The point is Granada, not all places once governed by Nasrid rulers. Cultural continuities after conquest are not represented as continued Nasrid sovereignty.

- [The Art of the Nasrid Period (1232–1492)](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-nasrid-period-1232-1492) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Describes the dynastic dates, Granada’s precarious politics and court arts.
- [Nasrid Rule in Spain](https://www.metmuseum.org/learn/educators/curriculum-resources/art-of-the-islamic-world/unit-five/chapter-one/nasrid-rule-in-spain) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Explains continued use of the Alhambra and Andalusian forms after 1492.

### Makuria: a Christian kingdom along the middle Nile

ID: `makuria-kingdom`. Displayed interval: 550–1400. Region: africa.

Approximate c. 550–1400 follows the archaeological project’s fifth/sixth–fourteenth-century Makurian period, rounded to annual display. Christianization, contraction and royal fragmentation had different dates. This is a qualified dynastic-period convention, not the end of Nubian peoples or Christianity.

- [Historical city travel guide: Kulubnarti, Sudan, late 12th century](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/historical-city-travel-guide-kulubnarti-sudan-late-12th-century) — British Museum / Julie Renee Anderson. Curator account of Makuria, Tungul, Christianity, Nile economy and relations with Egypt.
- [Dongola archaeological project](https://pcma.uw.edu.pl/en/2019/04/02/dongola-2/) — Polish Centre of Mediterranean Archaeology, University of Warsaw. Project summary dates the Makurian phase fifth/sixth–fourteenth century; indexed text reviewed, full page presented a captcha.

### Almoravids: linking the Sahara, Morocco and al-Andalus

ID: `almoravid-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1040–1147. Region: africa.

1040–1147 is a broad movement-to-dynasty political convention. The Met art essay uses c. 1062–1150, with a narrower focus on Marrakesh and later artistic production; these conventions are not exact border dates.

- [The Art of the Almoravid and Almohad Periods](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-almoravid-and-almohad-periods-ca-1062-1269) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Explains Marrakesh, al-Andalus expansion and trans-Saharan exchange.
- [Medina of Marrakesh](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/331/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Describes Almoravid foundation and succeeding dynastic importance of Marrakesh.
- [Almoravid empire — Getty Thesaurus of Geographic Names](https://www.getty.edu/vow/TGNFullDisplay?english=Y&find=7142511&nation=&place=&subjectid=8711725) — Getty Research Institute. Institutional historical-place record dates the empire 1040–1147.

### Almohads: reform and empire in the western Mediterranean

ID: `almohad-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1130–1269. Region: africa.

1130–1269 follows a broad political convention beginning with Abd al-Mu’min’s leadership, rather than the movement’s earliest preaching. Losses in al-Andalus occurred earlier than the dynasty’s final defeat at Marrakesh.

- [The Art of the Almoravid and Almohad Periods](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-almoravid-and-almohad-periods-ca-1062-1269) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Explains Almohad rise, architecture and western Mediterranean reach.
- [Rabat, Modern Capital and Historic City](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1401/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Documents Almohad monumental construction and the historical layers of Rabat.
- [Almohad — Art and Architecture Thesaurus](https://www.getty.edu/vow/AATFullDisplay?find=archaeological+Park&logic=AND&note=&subjectid=300021528) — Getty Research Institute. Institutional authority record gives 1130–1269 for the North African and Iberian dynasty.

### Dahomey: royal compounds and a changing Atlantic world

ID: `dahomey-kingdom`. Displayed interval: 1625–1894. Region: africa.

1625–1894 is a qualified independent-kingdom interval; UNESCO’s palace succession runs to 1900 and must not be mistaken for sovereignty lasting to that date. The 1890s conquest and later dynastic continuity are distinct.

- [Royal Palaces of Abomey](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/323/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Describes successive royal compounds, reliefs and royal succession 1625–1900.
- [Royal palaces of Abomey](https://smarthistory.org/royal-palaces-abomey/) — Smarthistory / UNESCO. Explains the royal heritage in historical context.
- [The conquest of Dahomey (1890–1894)](https://www.servicehistorique.sga.defense.gouv.fr/en/thematic-topics/conquest-dahomey-1890-1894) — Service historique de la Defense, France. Archival history dates French conquest to 1890–1894; read as an archive of the conquering power, not a neutral account of all experiences.

### Ayyubids: a family of courts from Egypt to Syria

ID: `ayyubid-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1171–1260. Region: africa.

1171–1260 follows the Met’s broad dynastic period. Egypt’s independent Ayyubid line ended in 1250; Syrian strongholds had another trajectory. Cairo is a representative early center, not the court of every ruler.

- [The Art of the Ayyubid Period](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-ayyubid-period-ca-1171-1260) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Explains Salah al-Din’s rise, Egypt-Syria chronology and urban artistic connections.
- [The Art of the Mamluk Period](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-mamluk-period-1250-1517) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Distinguishes Egypt’s 1250 transition from later Syrian succession.

### Purépecha state: a western rival to the Mexica

ID: `purepecha-state`. Displayed interval: 1250–1532. Region: latin-america.

1250–1532 follows the Met’s broad Tarascan-state chronology. Archaeological studies may date a more centralized imperial phase later. Tzintzuntzan is a representative capital, not a fixed boundary for the whole interval.

- [Mexico, 1400–1600 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/08/canm.html) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Dates the Tarascan state 1250–1532 alongside contemporary Mesoamerican states.
- [Tarascan Ceramic Production and Implications for Ceramic Distribution](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/abs/tarascan-ceramic-production-and-implications-for-ceramic-distribution/4A4AD52EBB7B2DCB106DA8FAE4C30402) — Helen P. Pollard / Ancient Mesoamerica. Archaeological research on state economic coordination and ceramic production.
- [Time and Space in Tzintzuntzan](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5251) — Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, Mexico. Official site interpretation identifies the capital and its material evidence.

### Mixtec kingdoms: alliances recorded in painted books

ID: `mixtec-kingdoms`. Displayed interval: 1000–1521. Region: latin-america.

Approximate 1000–1521 represents Postclassic kingdoms before Spanish conquest, following the Met. Individual kingdoms began and changed at different dates; 1521 is a regional conquest-period convention, not the end of all Indigenous authority.

- [Mexico, 1400–1600 A.D.](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/08/canm.html) — The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Groups Mixtec and Zapotec kingdoms c. 1000–1521.
- [Mixtec Landscape Perception and Archaeological Settlement Patterns](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/8063/) — Bruce E. Byland and John M. D. Pohl / scholarly archaeological research. Combines settlement surveys and codices as evidence for interacting elite domains.
- [Mixtec Group Codices](https://research.famsi.org/research/pohl/jpcodices/pohlmixtec1.html) — John Pohl / Foundation for the Advancement of Mesoamerican Studies. Explains genealogies, political history and place signs in Mixtec codices.

### Toltec Tula: a capital and its contested wider reach

ID: `toltec-tula-state`. Displayed interval: 900–1150. Region: latin-america.

Approximate 900–1150 is INAH’s Tollan urban phase, not a securely bounded empire lifespan. The state is represented by its documented capital; broader political reach remains disputed.

- [Examining Tula’s Complex Urban Layout](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5542) — Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, Mexico. Dates the Tollan phase 900–1150 and explains city extent and urban planning.
- [INAH finds a Toltec residential unit at Tula](https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/arqueologos-del-inah-hallan-unidad-residencial-de-origen-tolteca-en-tula-hidalgo) — Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, Mexico. Research announcement records a 900–1150 residential unit beyond the ceremonial core.

### Champa: coastal courts and sacred centers

ID: `champa-kingdoms`. Displayed interval: 192–1832. Region: southeast-asia.

192–1832 is a broad sequence from the traditionally recorded first polity to the annexation of Panduranga. My Son’s main surviving building sequence is fourth–thirteenth century; it was not the capital for this entire interval. This is a plural polity lineage, not one unchanged state.

- [My Son Sanctuary](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/949/) — UNESCO World Heritage Centre. Describes the 192 origin tradition and the fourth–thirteenth-century sacred center, local adaptation and Cham architecture.
- [The My Son and Po Nagar Nha Trang Sanctuaries](https://ari.nus.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/wps08_100.pdf) — Tran Ky Phuong and Rie Nakamura / Asia Research Institute, National University of Singapore. Research paper distinguishes north and south Cham sacred centers and connects monuments with living communities.
- [The destruction and assimilation of Campa (1832–35) as seen from Cam sources](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-studies/article/abs/destruction-and-assimilation-of-campa-183235-as-seen-from-cam-sources/3BE62801898A772227D5E66235245065) — Nicolas Weber / Journal of Southeast Asian Studies. Research based on Cham sources examines annexation and assimilation beginning in 1832.

### Konbaung: royal expansion and colonial conquest

ID: `konbaung-dynasty`. Displayed interval: 1752–1885. Region: southeast-asia.

1752–1885 is the conventional dynastic interval. Mandalay was only the final capital, not the dynasty’s capital from its beginning. The endpoint marks overthrow of the monarchy, not an end to resistance or community histories.

- [Burma to Myanmar: 1500 years of connection and isolation](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/burma-myanmar-1500-years-connection-and-isolation) — British Museum. Curatorial history identifies Konbaung foundation in 1752 and conflict with British expansion; full page fetch was blocked, indexed excerpt reviewed.
- [Burmese Sources for the History of the Konbaung Period 1752–1885](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/7D086F4B750FBBD52BA7F870E5E22E98/S0217781100002477a.pdf/burmese_sources_for_the_history_of_the_konbaung_period_17521885.pdf) — Ma Yi Yi / Journal of Southeast Asian History. Primary-source study defines the period and surveys Burmese historical records.

### Kamakura: warrior government beside the imperial court

ID: `kamakura-shogunate`. Displayed interval: 1185–1333. Region: east-asia.
