# V10 Africa and West/Central Asia: research and editorial ledger

Reviewed 2026-10-23. Data: `dist/data/expansion-africa-west-asia-v10.json`.

## Scope and result

This contribution contains **24 new records: 9 biographies and 15 connected histories**, with **41 separately dated and located biographical milestones**. Fifteen records use the `africa` region and nine use `west-central-asia`. Five biographies concern women. The non-biographical records comprise six events, five cultural histories and four places. None is a polity or declares a timeline bar. Counts describe this selection, not regional completeness or historical importance.

English biographies are 209–234 words and the historical narratives are 182–192 words, excluding dates, facts and milestones. Both languages retain the same uncertainty and scope. A few biographies remain slightly below the approximate 220-word drafting target rather than adding unsupported life episodes. The selection brings household documents, education, labour, property, displacement and book-making into connection with existing state and city histories.

The 42 distinct cited URLs were opened and their relevant contents inspected. Inspection means the particular text described below, not examination of every page linked by an institution or every original archival document. Thirty-eight URLs supply inspected article, reference-entry or object-catalog text; one supplies an inspected scholarly abstract; one supplies inspected archival catalog background; two supply inspected manuscript catalog descriptions. Retrieval labels preserve those distinctions. No failed page or search-result-only page is represented as inspected full text.

The record metadata uses release 4, edition `v10`, and `inspected-source-synthesis`. This describes the research activity performed, not a certification of every inference or of independent corroboration. Each source has a claim-specific `evidenceNote` and review date. Interpretive sentences explain what the evidence permits readers to ask; they do not supply new dates, quantities or routes.

## Connected sequences and deduplication

Stable IDs and bilingual titles were checked against the existing narrative, people and expansion arrays before finalization. All 24 IDs are new. Related links resolve to existing records or records in this contribution, with at least two per entry. No V9 entry was rewritten and no source import was relabeled.

- **Nile and Maghrib scholarship:** Ibn Khaldun, Ibn al-Haytham and the Cairo Geniza deepen existing Fatimid/Mamluk/Arabic-learning contexts. They do not repeat those political overviews. The Geniza is a documentary-survival entry, distinct from a new city-founding claim.
- **Egyptian organizing:** Shaarawi’s personal career is paired with the 1919–1924 collective sequence. The latter follows national mobilization, the women’s union and exclusion from institutions. It is not a second record for the unveiling gesture.
- **Southern African institutions and lives:** Maxeke, Head and Makeba connect education, political organization and literary/music careers to the Land Act, the 1956 march and Sophiatown. The three events address different mechanisms of racial control; the links do not make the 1913 statute the direct cause of every later removal.
- **West African Atlantic histories:** Freetown’s 1792 and 1800 migration moments connect to existing Atlantic slavery/emancipation records. This adds a local settlement conflict rather than duplicating a generic trade route. It explicitly recognizes prior African communities and differences between the migrant groups.
- **Nubian and Swahili local histories:** Faras and Lamu add religious, domestic and craft contexts to existing Makuria, Kilwa, Songo Mnara and Zanzibar entries. Faras is not a duplicate of the Abu Simbel rescue entry; the link allows comparison of displacement and preservation.
- **West and Central Asian households and arts:** Kanesh’s tablets, the Sogdian letters, Afrasiab, Merv, Herat and Ardabil distinguish letters, representations, urban phases and made objects. They supplement, rather than repeat, Uruk writing, Samarkand, Seljuk/Timurid/Safavid overviews and Isfahan.
- **Literature, printing and citizenship:** Navoi and Halide Edib are new biographies. Herat’s early fifteenth-century workshop predates Navoi’s later career. The 1729 dictionary is a bounded publication event, not the origin of Ottoman printing or a blanket claim about Islam and technology.

Biographies already present—including Mansa Musa, Ibn Battuta, Njinga, Nana Asma’u, Taytu Betul, Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, Albertina Sisulu, Nelson Mandela, Wangari Maathai, al-Biruni, Ulugh Beg, Tahirih and Soraya Tarzi—were retained as existing reading connections rather than duplicated.

## Decisions that materially affect interpretation

**Biographical anchors.** Ibn Khaldun’s 1377 node is an initial draft, followed by revision, not a single immutable book publication. Ibn al-Haytham’s 965 and 1040 nodes are conventional approximate dates; his 1027 intellectual autobiography and 1038 Egyptian evidence are firmer anchors. The record does not manufacture dates from the disputed Nile-engineering story. Maxeke’s birth year/place discrepancies and the SAHO profile’s problematic identification of Wilberforce with Cleveland are not resolved by inventing a birth pin. The profile uses career anchors and correctly locates the university in Ohio at narrative scale.

Head’s 1968 publication uses Francistown as the novel’s drafting context; later publication points use Serowe. These explicitly do not mean a publisher’s city or presence on publication day. The biography includes her Radisele and Francistown experiences without an invented dated itinerary. The 1968 first-publication year follows Botswana Writers; SAHO gives 1969 and the discrepancy remains explicit. Makeba’s 1960 New York point is an overseas-career reference, not the site of the passport authority; her 1990 Johannesburg point is a homecoming context, not a verified airport. Abd al-Qadir’s 1832 starting year is a public-career choice, and the 1847 surrender marker is regional. Amboise’s later captivity and release announcement are distinguished from the surrender itself. The sources disagree over the exact December day, which is not stated. His 1860 protection of Christians is located in Damascus; the record does not adopt a precise rescue total or convert commemorative praise into an assertion of universal personal virtue.

**Halide Edib and Antoura.** The Turkish government and university profiles illuminate education, writing, 1919 rallies and parliamentary work but omit coercive wartime institutional history. The Armenian Genocide Museum-Institute researcher’s account and Near East Relief’s archival interpretation explicitly connect her to the administration/educational responsibilities of Antoura. The record includes forced assimilation of Armenian children during the genocide, while avoiding an unsupported claim that every abuse was personally ordered or carried out by her. The photograph dates 1919–1920, not her service. Conflicting orphan counts, a single-year Antoura milestone and a precise itinerary through the school system are held. The two celebratory profiles disagree on the years abroad, so the narrative leaves that interval undated.

**Objects and words.** Navoi’s 1485 milestone is the final year of the 1483–1485 composition span, not an approximate birth-like date; the span remains explicit. The later 1580 divan is evidence of reception. Kanesh’s two objects are cataloged as *probably* from Kültepe, not excavated at identified domestic addresses. The Sogdian letters are described through catalog text rather than newly checked transcriptions. Samarkand is the named recipient city of Letter 2, while the manuscripts’ find context is near Dunhuang. The map does not imply either letter arrived.

**Institutions and material settings.** Afrasiab’s lost centre and room function remain debated, and costume identification is not proof of a literal embassy roster. The 650–700 window is broad; no precise coronation or painting year is adopted. Herat distinguishes original literary composition, fifteenth-century copying and later binding. Merv’s 1000–1200 focus does not make all adjacent walled cities contemporary or assign Sanjar’s full reign to a building project. Ardabil marks the shrine association of a dated carpet, not a securely identified workshop. Maqsud Kashani’s named role is probable supervision; deferential inscription wording does not by itself prove literal enslavement.

**Collective histories.** The 1913 Land Act record follows the law and later allocation extension to 1936, rather than asserting instant, uniform dispossession. The contextual caution comes from Beinart and Delius’s inspected abstract, not an unreviewed claim to all their case studies. The 1956 march attendance remains an estimate of roughly 10,000–20,000, and the protest is not treated as immediate abolition. Sophiatown’s 1955 focus marks the beginning of removals. Faras’s medieval painting dates are distinct from modern excavation and museum transfer. Lamu’s nineteenth-century focus neither dates all surviving buildings nor ends its living traditions.

## Source-family independence and limitations

The number of URLs is not the number of independent witnesses. Met object pages for Kanesh and Herat share a curatorial source family. The two Sogdian descriptions are from the same British Library catalog. V&A, Princeton Geniza, Faras, Lamu, Merv, Afrasiab and the printing history each have a deliberately bounded narrative supported by one principal inspected source family. Their entries do not claim independent corroboration of every interpretation.

SAHO is a substantial recurring source for the southern African selection. It is not counted as an independent corroboration of itself when a biography is reused for a context entry. The US National Archives guide, DACB’s church-history account, Botswana Writers, South African government remembrance and the research abstract add distinct institutional or scholarly perspectives, but possible underlying dependence has not been exhaustively traced. The government remembrance of the march is independent publication, not a separate attendance census. The UNB finding aid is an archivist’s historical context, not direct reading of every Colonial Office manuscript.

The Navoi university page is derivative and credits tafakkur.net; the government commemoration is also a national literary presentation. They are not two independent specialist biographies. A Met manuscript record independently supports his life dates, literary role and later copying, but does not independently verify every office or endowment. Prodigy anecdotes, exact construction totals and the government page’s conflation of Khorasan with Transoxiana were excluded. Modern national literary importance is kept distinct from the multilingual Timurid setting.

Khaldun’s scholarly reference and Turkish specialist encyclopedia offer more than a generic homepage but remain reference syntheses. Ibn al-Haytham’s St Andrews profile explicitly reports uncertain traditions, while the UNESCO article concentrates on scientific work; its sole-inventor rhetoric is not adopted. The Abd al-Qadir heritage and interfaith commemorations are used for specified facts and read alongside a specialist reference entry. The Halide Edib source families are intentionally different in institutional purpose; the conflicting perspectives are not treated as mutually cancelling the supported coercive history.
