# Indus, religious and Indigenous histories · 27 October 2026

This continuation adds five original bilingual histories and three linked reading paths. It preserves the released narrative inputs and 658 historical map instances; it does not add or infer archaeological, religious or Indigenous territorial boundaries. The [baseline](INDUS_RELIGIONS_INDIGENOUS_BASELINE_2026_10_27.json) pins the previously released V14 files, map manifest and prior course output. A source review is not a community or specialist endorsement.

## Indus civilization

- `indus-civilization-urban-networks` places mature urbanism approximately **2600–1900 BCE** and connects the earlier individual Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira entries. [Green and Petrie’s research article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7665814/) was directly inspected in Chrome; it analyzes varied settlement distributions and discusses the possibility that major urban centers had distinct political arrangements. [Kenoyer’s Mohenjo-daro overview](https://www.harappa.com/slideshows/mohenjo-daro) was inspected during the earlier site review. Neither source makes shared styles proof of a single government. The representative Mohenjo-daro point does not claim a region-wide boundary.
- `indus-post-urban-transformations` uses **1900–1600 BCE** as the selected portion of Green and Petrie’s archaeological analysis. Some parts of their northwestern Indian study area show increased numbers of often smaller, dispersed sites after 1900 BCE; patterns differ by locality, and the authors describe uneven survey coverage and other data limits. This is evidence of varied transformation, not a one-day collapse or disappearance of people. The study-area point is an orientation only.
- Green and Petrie’s inspected text did not substantiate a claim about the decipherment of the Indus signs; that claim was not added from this source. Earlier pre-urban phases, the interpretation of scripts and regional changes beyond the paper’s scope remain follow-up work.

## Religious traditions

- `hinduism-living-traditions` concerns **contemporary Hindu communities** rather than a date of origin. The visible [Harvard Pluralism Project Hinduism hub](https://pluralism.org/hinduism) describes Greater Boston communities worshipping and celebrating in homes and rented spaces, later temples, and contemporary centers. The expanded historical chronology was not accessible in this session and was not used. Cambridge identifies the research project, not a religious origin; the 2000–2026 window is an editorial contemporary focus. The page cannot represent all Hindu beliefs or communities.
- `course-religious-traditions-evidence` connects existing, previously reviewed Vedic, Zoroastrian, Jain, Buddhist, Aksumite Christian, Islamic, Jewish and Sikh political contexts with this contemporary Hindu entry. Its chronological anchors are chosen within each entry's own focus interval, not dates of religious origins or evidence that all traditions were present at each anchor. The [Princeton Geniza Project FAQ](https://geniza.princeton.edu/en/about/faq/), previously inspected for the Cairo Geniza, concerns particular preserved texts and a Jewish communal setting; it does not establish a complete history of Judaism. The existing Sikh kingdom entry concerns a political period, not the origins or end of the Sikh religion. Direct histories of early Judaism, Christianity, Buddhism and Sikh traditions remain gaps requiring stronger accessible specialist and community sources.

## Indigenous North America

- `sivullirmiut-early-arctic-history` draws on the directly inspected Inuit-section account in the [Indigenous Peoples Atlas of Canada](https://indigenouspeoplesatlasofcanada.ca/article/early-history/). It describes Sivullirmiut, several archaeological labels, an eastward movement **around 5,000 years ago**, Inuit Elders’ accounts and present-day Inuit life. The single **approximately 3000 BCE** map anchor translates that broad statement; it is not a precisely dated migration, a duration or a disappearance. The page supplies no end date for Sivullirmiut or Dorset, no date for Thule arrival and no archaeological evidence that overlapping groups necessarily met. Different interpretations and community perspectives remain important.
- `indigenous-north-america-nations-continuity` compares **distinct** Haudenosaunee, Leech Lake Ojibwe and Kwakwaka’wakw accounts. It reuses the previously inspected [Smithsonian community-informed Haudenosaunee guide](https://americanindian.si.edu/sites/1/files/pdf/education/HaudenosauneeGuide.pdf), [Leech Lake Ojibwe account](https://americanindian.si.edu/environment/ojibwe/GetStarted.cshtml) and [U’mista Cultural Centre’s potlatch history](https://www.umista.ca/pages/collection-history); the prior review dates and retrieval status are retained in the record. U’mista did not successfully open during this session, so this is **not** represented as a fresh inspection. The 1400–2026 window is editorial, not the founding of a nation or a single territorial history.
- The Indigenous reading connects archaeological places, Indigenous knowledge, recent people and living communities. It is neither an itinerary nor an assertion that all its communities share a single ancestry or political system. Further regional coverage and review led by the relevant Indigenous communities remain needed, especially beyond the examples already represented.
